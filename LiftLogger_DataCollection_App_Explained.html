Why this comes first

No data, no model

A neural network only learns from examples. For "what exercise is this?", an example means: a chunk of wrist motion paired with the correct label.

Nothing off-the-shelf gives you that. So before any AI, you have to build the thing that captures it — a recorder that knows both what your wrist did and what you told it you were doing.

Everything in this deck exists to produce two clean files: readings.csv (the motion) and sets.csv (the labels). Those are the raw material the AI deck picks up.

The shape of it

Two apps that talk to each other

The watch records. The phone collects. You hand the result to the laptop. This deck is the first two boxes.

Inside Xcode

One project, several targets

A "target" is one buildable thing. This project bundles a few, and the watch app is officially a companion of the phone app — that link is what lets the phone push the watch app over the air.

The iPhone app. Bundle ID Apple.LiftLogger, runs iOS 26.5.

⌚ LiftLogger Watch App

The watch app. Bundle ID ...watchkitapp; its "companion" is set to the phone app.

🧪 Test targets

Unit + UI test bundles Xcode generates automatically. We didn't touch these.

⚙️ Capabilities

HealthKit permission strings + a "workout" background mode — both needed for recording (next slides).

The watch app · what the user does

A simple three-state flow

Start a session, tap the exercise you're about to do (that begins a "set"), hit End Set when done, repeat, then End Session. Simple on purpose — it has to be usable mid-workout.

The watch app · reading motion

Six numbers, 50 times a second

Using Apple's Core Motion framework, the app asks the watch for a motion update every 1/50th of a second. Each update gives six values:

Acceleration — x, y, z

How hard the wrist is speeding up in each direction. We keep gravity in the signal — it reveals how your arm is tilted, which helps tell exercises apart.

Rotation — x, y, z

How fast the wrist is twisting around each axis (the gyroscope).

Together those six channels are a rich fingerprint of the movement. 50× per second is fast enough to capture the shape of a rep, slow enough not to flood storage.

The watch app · what gets written

The two files, created live

① readings.csv — the firehose

subject,session,time_ms,
acc_x,acc_y,acc_z,gyro_x,gyro_y,gyro_z
S01,2026..,103.2,0.04,0.98,0.11,0.5,-0.2,0.1
# a new row 50× per second

② sets.csv — the labels

subject,session,exercise,start_ms,end_ms
S01,2026..,bicep_curl,5021.0,17030.0
# one row each time you
# tap an exercise then End Set

As you record, sensor rows stream into the first file; every set you mark appends one row to the second. Both are plain text — openable in any spreadsheet.

The watch app · the detail that makes labeling possible

Everything stamped with one clock

Both the 50-per-second sensor rows and your "start set / end set" taps are timestamped from the same source: the watch's time since it last booted, in milliseconds.

That's deliberate. Because they share a clock, the laptop can later say with certainty: "this motion row happened inside that bicep-curl set" — just by comparing numbers. No drift, no guesswork.

This is the single hinge the whole project swings on. If the two files used different clocks, every label would be off by seconds and the data would be useless. The app was written specifically so they always agree.

The watch app · a clever necessity

Keeping it alive with the screen off

Normally watchOS suspends an app the moment the screen sleeps — which would kill your recording mid-set. The fix is a trick:

  • The app quietly starts a HealthKit workout session (strength-training, indoor) the instant you tap Start Session.
  • watchOS keeps workout apps running with the screen dark — so the sensors keep streaming through your whole set.
  • That's why the app asks for Health permission and declares a workout-processing background mode — purely to stay awake, not to log to the Health app.
  • When you end the session it discards that workout, so it doesn't clutter your Activity rings.

A non-obvious but essential piece: without it, you'd have to keep your wrist raised and the screen on for every rep.

The watch app · not losing data

Written safely, a second at a time

One worker, in order

All file-writing happens on a single dedicated queue, so two updates can never scribble over each other — even at 50 writes a second.

Buffer & flush

Rows are gathered in memory and written to disk roughly once per second. If the app ever crashed, you'd lose at most ~1 second of motion, never the whole session.

Small touches, but they're the difference between "we have the data" and "the file was half-written and corrupt."

The watch app · handing off

Sending the files to the phone

When you tap End Session, the watch closes both files and ships them to the iPhone over Watch Connectivity (Apple's watch↔phone link). Each file is tagged with which session and subject it belongs to.

  • Delivered safely: the watch only deletes its local copy once the phone confirms it arrived.
  • "Resend" button: if the phone was out of range or the app closed early, the files wait on the watch and you can re-send them later — nothing is lost.

Transfers can take a minute after End Session — the status text on the watch tells you what's happening so it never feels frozen.

The iPhone app · the collector

Catch, file, and label by subject

The phone app's job is bookkeeping. As recordings arrive from the watch it:

  • Receives each file and tucks it into a per-session folder (sessions/<date-time>/).
  • Lists every session it has, with file sizes, so you can see what you've collected.
  • Sets the subject ID (e.g. S01) and pushes it to the watch, so each person's recordings are tagged with who they are — vital for training fairly across people.
  • Lets you delete or share any individual session.

The iPhone app · the payoff button

Merge everything into two files

One tap concatenates every session into a single readings.csv and a single sets.csv — the exact two files the training pipeline expects. Then a Share button AirDrops them straight to your Mac.

That hand-off is the seam between this deck and the AI deck.

Part 2

Getting it onto
real hardware

Writing the app is half the battle. The other half — the part we actually spent our time on — is convincing Xcode, an iPhone, and an Apple Watch to trust each other.

Setup · where does it run?

Simulator vs. a real device

Xcode's toolbar has a run destination picker. Three kinds of things show up there, and the difference tripped us up:

Simulators

Fake iPhones/watches on your Mac. Great for the UI — but they have no real motion sensors, so useless for collecting data.

"Ryan's phone"

Your actual plugged-in iPhone. This is the one to pick — it installs the app for real and pushes the watch app to your watch.

"Any iOS Device"

A trap. It's for packaging the app, not running it — picking it gives a "build only" message and nothing launches.

The fix for the "build only device" error was simply choosing the real phone instead of "Any iOS Device."

Setup · proving it's you

Code signing

Apple won't let an app onto a real device unless it's cryptographically signed by a registered developer. That's signing, and it's required even for a personal app.

  • In the target's Signing & Capabilities tab, "Automatically manage signing" is on, and a Team (your Apple ID) is selected.
  • We confirmed it directly in the project file: every target shares team C5GN87B9UT with automatic signing — so signing was not the blocker.
  • A free Apple ID works, with limits: apps expire after 7 days and you can have a few at a time. A paid account ($99/yr) removes that.

First install also asks you to "trust" your developer certificate on the iPhone, under Settings → General → VPN & Device Management.

Setup · unlocking the hardware

Developer Mode — on both devices

Modern iPhones and watches ship with developer features locked off. You have to switch Developer Mode on, and — the catch that cost us time — it's a separate toggle on each device.

iPhone

Settings → Privacy & Security → Developer Mode → On, then restart.

Apple Watch

Settings → Privacy & Security → Developer Mode → On. Turning it on for the phone does not turn it on for the watch.

The watch's Developer Mode toggle sometimes only appears after Xcode has tried to connect to it at least once — which is why it felt like it was missing.

Setup · the hardest part

Getting Xcode to see the watch

This is where we spent the most effort. The watch connects to Xcode wirelessly, through the phone — and that link is finicky. We hit two messages:

  • "Could not establish a connection / tunnel timed out" — Xcode found the watch but couldn't open its wireless channel. Cured by toggling Bluetooth/Wi-Fi, keeping the devices close and unlocked, and restarting both.
  • "May need to be unlocked to recover from preparation errors" — the watch literally needs to be unlocked on your wrist while Xcode prepares it.

The breakthrough realization: you don't need the watch tethered to Xcode at all. Only the iPhone has to be connected — it relays the watch app across automatically.

Setup · the version trap

Xcode, watchOS & the deployment target

Three version numbers have to line up, and one of them is almost certainly still blocking your install:

  • Xcode 26.5 must be new enough for watchOS 26.0.2 — it is. ✓
  • But the project's "deployment target" is set to 26.5 — meaning the app demands watchOS 26.5 or newer, while your watch only runs 26.0.2. The watch is "too old" for its own app.
The fix: in the Watch App target → General → Minimum Deployments, lower watchOS from 26.5 to 26.0 (or below). Then it will install. This is the kind of mismatch that produces a confusing "trait set" / install error even when signing and pairing are perfect.

Setup · the whole recipe, in order

Run-on-watch checklist

  1. Plug in the iPhone; unlock it; tap Trust This Computer.
  2. Turn on Developer Mode on the iPhone and the watch; restart both.
  3. In Xcode, set Team + automatic signing on every target.
  4. Lower the Watch App's deployment target to ≤ the watch's OS (26.0).
  5. Pick your real iPhone as the run destination — not a simulator, not "Any iOS Device."
  6. Keep the watch unlocked and nearby; press Run (⌘R). The phone relays the app to the watch.

Lessons worth keeping

What we learned the hard way

  • "Build only device" = wrong destination. Pick the real phone, not "Any iOS Device."
  • Developer Mode is per-device. The watch needs its own toggle, which may only appear after Xcode pokes it.
  • The watch connects through the phone. Don't fight to tether the watch directly — just connect the iPhone.
  • Unlock the watch while Xcode prepares it, or it stalls.
  • Deployment target can't exceed the watch's OS. The most likely remaining blocker — lower it to 26.0.

Where this leaves you

You've built a

Watch records six motion channels at 50 Hz, marks your sets, survives a screen-off workout, and ships clean labeled files to the phone — which merges them into the exact two CSVs the model needs.

Once it's running on the watch and you've recorded real sessions, the AI Training deck picks up exactly here.
